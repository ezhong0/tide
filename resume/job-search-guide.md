## 8. Application Strategy - Where & How

### The Numbers Game

**Reality:** You need to apply to 100-150 companies to get 1-3 offers.

**Your application funnel:**
```
100 applications
  → 15 responses (15%)
    → 10 phone screens
      → 5 onsites
        → 2 offers
```

**What this means:** Every week, apply to 20-25 companies.

### Where to Apply

**Best sources for startup jobs:**

**1. YC Companies (Apply to 30-50)**
- Work at a Startup: https://www.workatastartup.com
- Filter for: SF Bay Area, Remote, Software Engineer
- YC companies value builders, not pedigree
- Apply to current batch + last 2 batches

**2. Startup Job Boards (Apply to 30-40)**
- Wellfound (AngelList Talent)
- LinkedIn (filter: "Startup, 1-50 employees")
- Hacker News Who's Hiring (monthly thread)
- Sequoia Scout (https://www.sequoiacap.com/jobs)

**3. AI-Specific Companies (Apply to 20-30)**
- Companies building AI dev tools
- Companies using AI for productivity
- AI infrastructure companies
- Filter for Seed-Series B, 10-100 employees

**4. Direct Applications (10-20)**
- Companies you actually want to work for
- Research founders on Twitter/LinkedIn
- Find their careers page
- Apply + send personalized email to founder/hiring manager

### How to Apply

**Option A: Quick Apply (for volume)**
- Use job board's built-in application
- Attach resume (PDF)
- Write 2-3 sentence cover note if required
- Track in spreadsheet
- **Time:** 5 minutes per company

**Option B: Personalized Application (for top choices)**
- Apply through job board
- Email founder/hiring manager directly
- Reference specific thing about their company
- Link to your GitHub
- Mention 1-2 relevant projects
- **Time:** 15-20 minutes per company

**Recommended split:** 80% Quick Apply, 20% Personalized

### Application Tracking

**Use a spreadsheet with these columns:**
- Company name
- Role title
- Application date
- Source (YC, Wellfound, LinkedIn, etc.)
- Status (Applied, Phone Screen, Onsite, Rejected, Offer)
- Contact person
- Next action
- Notes

**Why:** You'll forget which companies you applied to. Track everything.

### Application Best Practices

**Resume:**
- Always send PDF (not .doc)
- Name it: `Edward_Zhong_Resume.pdf` (not "resume.pdf")
- Use ATS version for job boards
- Use visual version for direct emails

**Cover Letter:**
- Most startups don't require it
- If required, keep it to 3 short paragraphs
- Reference specific thing about their company/product
- Mention 1 relevant project
- Don't repeat your resume

**Timing:**
- Apply Monday-Thursday (better response rates)
- Morning applications get more attention
- Batch applications (do 10-15 at once)

## 10. Cold Outreach & Referrals

### When to Do Cold Outreach

**Use cold outreach for:**
- Companies you really want to work for
- Small startups (10-30 people) where founders read emails
- Companies that don't have open roles posted
- After you've applied (follow-up email)

**Don't use cold outreach for:**
- Big companies (your email won't reach anyone)
- Companies with 100+ applicants (too much noise)

### How to Find Contact Info

**Find the hiring manager or founder:**
1. Check company's About page (founders listed)
2. LinkedIn search: "[Company name] + CTO" or "Engineering Manager"
3. Use Hunter.io or RocketReach to find email

**Email format is usually:**
- firstname@company.com
- firstname.lastname@company.com
- f.lastname@company.com

**Why this works:**
- Real weakness (not "I work too hard")
- Shows self-awareness
- Demonstrates growth
- Gives concrete example

### "Walk me through this project (assistantapp)"

**Use this structure:**

1. **Problem** (30 seconds): "I kept dropping commitments buried in email threads. Gmail search fails on complex queries like 'show me emails where I promised to follow up but never did.'"

2. **Solution** (1 minute): "I built a 3-layer DAG execution pipeline. Layer 1 uses an LLM to decompose queries into execution graphs with dependency resolution. Layer 2 has 5 execution strategies—metadata filtering, keyword search, semantic analysis, cross-reference, and batch thread reading—running in parallel stages for 2-5x speedup. Layer 3 synthesizes structured results back into natural language."

3. **Challenges** (30 seconds): "The hardest part was testing non-deterministic AI outputs. I built a framework using AI judges (GPT-5-nano and Claude evaluators)—generate realistic inboxes with ground truth, run queries, validate semantic correctness across multiple models."

4. **Results** (30 seconds): "74K lines of TypeScript across backend, frontend, and comprehensive tests. Production-grade with OAuth 2.0, AES-256-GCM encryption, circuit breakers. DataLoader pattern achieved 10x reduction in API calls. Not deployed yet, but the architecture is solid and the testing framework proves it works."

**Then ask:** "Want me to dive deeper into any part?"

### "Why do you want to work here?"

**Never say:** "I'm excited about the opportunity to learn"

**Always say:** Something specific about their product/tech/team

**Template:**

"I'm excited about [Company] for three reasons. First, [specific technical challenge they're solving]. Second, [something about their engineering culture/team from research]. Third, [how your background aligns]. For example, I built [relevant project] which is similar to [their product/tech]."

**Do your homework:**
- Read their blog posts
- Check their tech stack
- Find their engineering team on LinkedIn

<a name="interviews"></a>
## 13. Interview Preparation

### What to Expect

**Typical startup interview process:**

1. **Recruiter Screen (30 min)**
   - Tell me about yourself
   - Why are you looking?
   - Salary expectations
   - Logistics

2. **Technical Phone Screen (60 min)**
   - 1-2 LeetCode-style problems
   - Usually medium difficulty
   - Use Coderpad or HackerRank

3. **Onsite (3-5 hours, can be virtual)**
   - System design (1 hour)
   - 2-3 coding rounds (1 hour each)
   - Behavioral/cultural fit (30-60 min)
   - Team lunch or informal chat

4. **Offer or Rejection (1-5 days)**

### LeetCode Preparation

**Target:** 100-150 problems before you start interviewing heavily

**Strategy:**
- Focus on patterns, not memorization
- Do 2-3 problems per day
- Review problems you got wrong
- Time yourself (45 minutes per problem)

**Priority topics:**
1. Arrays & Strings (20 problems)
2. Hash Tables (15 problems)
3. Two Pointers (10 problems)
4. Trees (20 problems)
5. Graphs (15 problems)
6. Dynamic Programming (15 problems)
7. Backtracking (10 problems)

**Resources:**
- NeetCode.io (structured problem list)
- LeetCode Explore (interview prep)
- Blind 75 (curated list)

**Start NOW:** Even if you're not interviewing yet, do 1-2 problems per day.

### System Design Preparation

**You'll be asked things like:**
- "Design a URL shortener"
- "Design Twitter's timeline"
- "Design a chat application"

**Framework:**
1. **Clarify requirements** (5 min)
   - Functional requirements (what features?)
   - Non-functional requirements (scale? latency?)

2. **Back-of-envelope estimation** (5 min)
   - QPS (queries per second)
   - Storage needs
   - Bandwidth

3. **High-level design** (15 min)
   - Draw boxes and arrows
   - API design
   - Database schema

4. **Deep dive** (20 min)
   - Where interviewer wants to dig in
   - Usually: scaling, caching, or interesting algorithm

5. **Discuss tradeoffs** (10 min)
   - CAP theorem considerations
   - SQL vs NoSQL
   - Caching strategies

**Resources:**
- "System Design Interview" by Alex Xu (book)
- YouTube: Exponent, System Design Interview channel
- Practice with your AI assistant project (you designed it!)

**Your advantage:** You can talk about assistantapp architecture in detail with verified implementation specifics.

**Example technical deep-dive:**
- "Layer 2 execution coordinator implements 5 strategies: metadata-filter-strategy, keyword-search-strategy, semantic-analysis-strategy, cross-reference-strategy, and batch-thread-read-strategy"
- "Circuit breaker service uses 3 states (CLOSED, OPEN, HALF_OPEN) with configurable failure thresholds and recovery timeouts"
- "DataLoader pattern batches requests in same event loop tick using process.nextTick, reducing API calls from N individual requests to 1 batch request"
- "E2E evaluator uses multi-layer scoring: query understanding, retrieval quality, ranking quality, presentation quality, and overall success"

### Behavioral Interview Prep

**Common questions:**
- Tell me about a time you disagreed with a teammate
- Tell me about a project that failed
- Tell me about a time you had to learn something quickly
- Why do you want to work here?

**Use STAR format:**
- **Situation:** Set the context
- **Task:** What needed to be done
- **Action:** What YOU did
- **Result:** What happened (quantify if possible)

**Prepare 5-7 stories:**
- 2 from Integuru
- 2 from Bill.com
- 2-3 from projects (assistantapp, hackathons)

**Practice these out loud.**

### Questions to Ask (Always Have 2-3)

**Good questions:**
- "What's the biggest technical challenge the team is facing right now?"
- "How does the team handle technical debt?"
- "What does success look like for this role in 6 months?"
- "How do you balance moving fast with maintaining code quality?"
- "What's the team's approach to mentorship for newer engineers?"

**Don't ask:**
- Questions answered on their website
- "What does your company do?" (you should know)
- Only about perks/benefits (ask about engineering first)

## 16. Company Research

### How to Build Your Target List

**Start with these sources:**

**YC Companies:**
- Work at a Startup: workatastartup.com
- Filter: San Francisco, Remote, Software Engineer
- Target: Current batch + last 3 batches
- **Add 30-50 companies**

**Wellfound (AngelList):**
- Search: "Software Engineer" + "San Francisco" + "AI"
- Filter: Seed-Series B, 10-100 employees
- **Add 30-40 companies**

**Hacker News Who's Hiring:**
- Monthly thread: https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=[latest]
- CTRL+F: "AI", "TypeScript", "San Francisco"
- **Add 10-20 companies**

**Direct Research:**
- Companies you follow on Twitter
- Companies from TechCrunch articles
- Companies your friends work at
- **Add 10-20 companies**

### How to Prioritize

**Tier 1 (Top 20 - Personalized Applications):**
- You know someone there
- OR the product/mission really excites you
- OR the tech stack is perfect fit
- Apply with personalized email to founder/hiring manager

**Tier 2 (Next 30 - Quick Apply + Follow-Up):**
- Good fit but no connection
- Interesting product
- Reasonable tech stack
- Apply through job board, send follow-up email after 1 week

**Tier 3 (Remaining 50+ - Volume):**
- Decent fit
- Building AI products
- Hiring for your level
- Quick apply through job boards

### Company Evaluation Criteria

**Before you apply, check:**

**✅ Good signs:**
- Recent funding (last 12 months)
- 10-100 employees (still growing, not too chaotic)
- Engineering blog (shows they value technical depth)
- Open source contributions (good engineering culture)
- Strong founders (look at LinkedIn backgrounds)

**🚩 Red flags:**
- No funding in 2+ years (might be struggling)
- 1-2 employees (too early, too risky)
- 500+ employees (too corporate, not startup)
- No product yet (idea stage)
- Stealth mode with no details (can't evaluate)

### Research Before Interview

**When you get a phone screen, research:**

**30 minutes of research:**
1. Use their product (sign up, try features)
2. Read their blog (last 3-4 posts)
3. Check founder/CTO Twitter (recent tweets)
4. LinkedIn: Find engineers, see their backgrounds
5. Crunchbase: Funding, investors, timeline

**Prepare 2-3 questions based on research:**
- "I saw in your blog post about [X], how does that work with [Y]?"
- "I noticed you're using [tech stack], what made you choose that over [alternative]?"

## 17. Reality Check & Mindset

### This Will Be Hard

**You will:**
- Send 100+ applications
- Get rejected a lot
- Fail interviews you thought went well
- Get ghosted by companies
- Feel discouraged
- Question yourself

**This is normal. It happens to everyone.**

### What Success Looks Like

**You don't need:**
- A 100% hit rate
- To pass every interview
- To get offers from every company

**You only need:**
- 1-3 offers
- To find 1 great company
- To keep going when it's hard

### The Numbers Will Work

**If you:**
- Apply to 100-150 companies
- Do 100+ LeetCode problems
- Practice your story
- Prepare for system design
- Follow up on networking
- Keep going for 8-12 weeks

**You WILL get offers.**

It's a numbers game. Trust the process.

### When It Gets Discouraging

**Remember:**
- You have a 74K-line project with novel architecture that 99% of candidates don't have
- You implemented production patterns (OAuth, encryption, circuit breakers, DataLoader batching) from scratch
- You have production experience at Integuru serving 200+ customers
- You have a CS degree from UCSB
- You can build complex systems end-to-end
- You just need 1 company to say yes

**Reframe rejections:**
- "Not a fit" doesn't mean "not good enough"
- It means timing, team needs, budget, culture, or randomness
- Every rejection gets you closer to the right fit

### Support System

**Talk to:**
- Friends going through job search
- UCSB alumni who've been through this
- Hackathon contacts who are cheering for you
- Integuru colleagues who know your work

**Don't:**
- Isolate yourself
- Compare yourself to people with FAANG offers
- Let one bad interview ruin your week

### Final Advice

**Job searching is a full-time job:**
- Treat it like work (8 hours/day)
- Set daily goals (5 applications, 2 LeetCode, 3 networking messages)
- Track your progress
- Celebrate small wins

**Take care of yourself:**
- Sleep enough
- Exercise
- Take breaks
- Don't burn out before you get offers

**You've got this.**

You have the skills. You have the projects. You have the story.

Now execute the plan.
